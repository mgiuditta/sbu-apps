Thank you for the review. I'm not resubmitting Sbu Moon because I believe it meets 4.3 and 4.2.6. Answers below.

1. Sbu Moon shows the state of the moon for any day, computed on the device with the algorithms in Jean Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms, with no network at all. It opens on today's moon drawn as it actually looks, the illuminated percentage as the main figure, waxing or waning, the phase name, date and time of the next four principal phases, and the moon's age and distance. Swipe for the previous or next day, or pick any date between 1900 and 2100 from a calendar. Moonrise and moonset come either from your position, read once to about a kilometre (When In Use, and only after you tap "Use my location"), or from a built-in list of cities, shown in that city's own time when it differs from the phone's. Phase alerts are plain local notifications at an hour you choose. No account, no server of mine.

2. People who follow the moon for a practical reason: gardeners and anglers planning by the phase, night photographers and amateur astronomers who want the next full or new moon with its exact time, and anyone who just wants a full-moon alert.

3. The moon apps I studied are paid or ad-supported, several need the network to compute rise and set, and some keep location access on. Sbu Moon is free, has no ads, no account, no analytics, works fully offline including rise and set for a city, reads your location once and only on request, covers 1900 to 2100, and alerts at the hour you pick. It works with VoiceOver and at the largest Dynamic Type sizes.

4. Internal testing. TestFlight builds on my iPhone 15 Pro Max, through a written checklist with pass/fail results: today's moon, swiping days, the calendar at the 1900 and 2100 limits, location request and denial, the city list, alerts, largest text, VoiceOver, light and dark, Italian and English, relaunch; iPhone SE in the simulator. Rise and set are also unit-tested against United States Naval Observatory tables. No outside testers for 1.0. What testing changed: rise and set for a chosen city were shown in the phone's time zone, wrong for a city abroad, now in the city's own clock and labelled when different; the percent sign misaligned the figure at large text, now treated as the unit on the baseline; a precision pass caught wording and spacing problems on the phases list and alerts screen.

5. Standalone. It belongs to the small set of apps I publish as sbu.dev, each for one job in its own category: Moon, Habits (streaks), Level (level and clinometer), Noise (sleep sounds), Speed (GPS speedometer), Clean (freeing Photos space), and Sbu Scanner QR Barcode and Sbu Subscriptions, on the store since May and June 2026. One app per category. Moon is the only one that computes anything astronomical.

6. No. Nothing else I publish deals with the sky or with location-based times. Bundling it would add a location prompt and a city list to an app whose users never asked for either. And with all my apps free and without in-app purchases, a paid feature expansion isn't possible.

7. Yes: SbuKit, a Swift package I wrote and share across my apps, about 1,700 lines. It's the design system (colour and type tokens, button styles, the big-number view, the promise pills) plus a Settings screen with the privacy policy and font licence. No astronomical or business logic. Moon's own code is about 2,300 lines, tests against USNO included.

8. None. Only Apple frameworks: SwiftUI, Core Location, UserNotifications. The algorithms come from a published textbook and I implemented them myself. The one outside asset is the Bricolage Grotesque typeface (SIL OFL), credited in Settings.

9. No. I'm Matteo Giuditta, an individual developer. I designed and wrote Sbu Moon in Swift and SwiftUI, in my own repository, without a template, an app generation service, or anyone else's content, concept or branding. sbu.dev is my own site.
